Fukutokucho Shurui Co., Ltd. (headquartered in Matsudo, Chiba Prefecture, just east of Tokyo), part of the major Japanese brewer Oenon Group, has added a large 3,000 ml size to the tanrei karakuchi (light and dry) type of its ‘Fukutokucho Suutto Nomete Yasashii Osake’ series, on sale nationwide from 28 September 2026. The aim is clear: to deliver an everyday sake for the ‘nightly glass’ in a bigger and cheaper format.

## Following the success of the 1.8-litre size

The product is a sake brewed from 100% domestically grown rice and natural water, in a tanrei karakuchi style: light, crisp and dry. It is marketed as a sake that can be drunk chilled, at room temperature or warmed. The 1,800 ml size (traditionally known in Japan as an isshobin) was launched first, in November 2025, and its popularity for both flavour and price led to the even larger 3,000 ml size now joining the range. Drinking sake with dinner is a deeply rooted habit in Japanese households, and for products aimed at these daily drinkers, the amount you get for the price is what decides the choice.

## A distinctive method called ‘shunrei-zukuri’

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/17502253/pexels-photo-17502253.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Rice used for brewing (illustrative image)" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Rice used for brewing (illustrative image) — Photo: Mari M (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

Common to the whole series is a method the company calls ‘shunrei-zukuri’ (instant-chill brewing). During brewing, heat is applied to the rice for a moment and followed immediately by rapid cooling, which is said to draw out the rice’s umami while achieving a light mouthfeel. The brewing takes place in a region of natural beauty with views of the Southern Alps and Mount Fuji. What the press release stresses is not a grade such as junmai or ginjo, but the combination of easy drinking, true to the name’s ‘suutto’ (smoothly), and an affordable price, which shows clearly where Japan’s everyday sake market is being fought.

## For readers in Europe

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/39692059/pexels-photo-39692059.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Sake serving vessels (illustrative image)" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Sake serving vessels (illustrative image) — Photo: Szymon Shields (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

A 3-litre sake is unlikely ever to be exported. Yet this news tells us something about what people in Japan actually drink day to day. Sake shelves in Europe tend to be filled with premium bottles, but most domestic consumption in Japan is of this kind of ‘sake for the nightly dinner table’. It helps to think of it as the equivalent of table wine or bag-in-box wine. If you come across an affordable, light and dry sake, try it not only chilled but, as the colder season approaches, as nurukan (lukewarm, about 40 degrees). Warming a light, dry sake lets the rice flavour swell and makes it a better match for stews and cheese. Once you recognise that ‘sake is not only about luxury bottles’, the ways of enjoying it open up considerably.
