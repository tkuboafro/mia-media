Single Malt Komagatake, the whisky made in Miyada village, in the mountains of Nagano Prefecture in central Japan, has made a major change of course. It is produced by Hombo Shuzo, which has its head office in Kagoshima. Until now the blend changed every year and only a limited number of bottles were released, but it is now on sale throughout the year. According to the Shinano Mainichi Shimbun, a local newspaper in Nagano Prefecture, the distillery has been expanding its production facilities bit by bit to make this possible, and aims to give every release the same aroma and flavour.

## From "a limited release each year" to "a standard you can buy any time"

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/9198299/pexels-photo-9198299.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Single malt whisky being poured into a glass" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Single malt whisky being poured into a glass — Photo: cottonbro studio (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

A single malt is a whisky made at a single distillery using only malted barley. Its appeal lies in the way each distillery's character comes through directly. Until now, Komagatake was a "one-off bottle", with a different combination of whiskies each year. That was interesting for collectors, but fans could not enjoy the same taste a second time. Selling year-round requires a stable supply of whisky and a flavour that does not drift from one batch to the next. This change of policy suggests the distillery is aiming for the next stage in both production volume and quality control.

## Aiming to be an everyday drink on the shelves of snack bars

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/17541188/pexels-photo-17541188.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Whisky bottles lined up on a bar shelf, evoking a drink you can always have at a regular haunt" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Whisky bottles lined up on a bar shelf, evoking a drink you can always have at a regular haunt — Photo: Thu Huynh (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

The phrase used in the article's headline, "a sight of bottles lined up in snack bars", captures the aim of this policy well. A Japanese "snack" is a small bar run by an owner known as the "mama". Regulars have a culture of "bottle keep", leaving their own bottle at the bar and drinking it little by little. What sits on the shelf is not a bottle for a special day but a familiar drink that can be topped up with the same brand whenever needed. One can read in this the wish to make Komagatake, which has been talked about as a limited release, a drink enjoyed in such everyday settings.

## How to enjoy it in Europe

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/7599324/pexels-photo-7599324.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Single malt poured into a tasting glass" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Single malt poured into a tasting glass — Photo: Michael  M (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

Japanese whisky is very popular, and in Europe it has tended to be scarce and expensive. Once it becomes a standard product in Japan, importers may find it easier to plan continuous purchasing. At specialist shops that stock Japanese whisky, check the label to see whether they have the year-round "Single Malt Komagatake" rather than a "yearly limited bottle". You could start by tasting it neat, then add a little water to see how the aroma opens up. The aim this time is for it to taste the same whenever you open it. Comparing two bottles from different years and judging that consistency for yourself is another pleasure that only a whisky that has become a standard can offer.
