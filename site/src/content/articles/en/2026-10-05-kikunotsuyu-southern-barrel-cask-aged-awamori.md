Kikunotsuyu Shuzo, a distillery on Miyakojima in Okinawa Prefecture, has regrouped its cask-aged awamori under a single series called Southern Barrel. The cask-aged koshu (aged awamori) and liqueurs that were previously sold separately now sit under one concept. Kikunotsuyu Koshu Southern Barrel, the flagship product launched in 1992, has had its packaging redesigned for the first time in 31 years. In addition, two new products, aged for nine years in brandy and sherry casks respectively, have joined the range in limited runs of 1,000 bottles each.

## The idea of polishing awamori in casks

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/36040787/pexels-photo-36040787.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Cask-aged spirit being poured into a glass" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Cask-aged spirit being poured into a glass — Photo: Edita Brus (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

Awamori is a traditional Okinawan distilled spirit made from rice and black koji (a mould that produces black spores). Miyakojima is a remote island south-west of the main island of Okinawa. According to Kikunotsuyu Shuzo, whisky and brandy are spirits born with cask ageing in mind, but Southern Barrel starts from a different place. Its foundation is the character of awamori itself: gentle on the palate and soft. The distillery explains that when awamori is rested in casks for a long time, the aroma of the wood blends in without sharp edges, producing a flavour that is distinctly awamori. The distillery presents this as "a new value for awamori".

Koshu Southern Barrel is characterised by the rich aroma that comes from oak casks and the mellow sweetness typical of the distillery. Its alcohol content is 25%. The new label expresses the sense of maturity in gold and is decorated with hibiscus, palm trees and Ryukyuan ornamental patterns. The contents, volume, strength and price are unchanged.

## Two limited editions: brandy casks and sherry casks

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/11560702/pexels-photo-11560702.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Two cask-aged spirits of different colours, with dried fruit" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Two cask-aged spirits of different colours, with dried fruit — Photo: Mathias Reding (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

The new "SOUTHERN BARREL Brandy note" is aged for nine years in brandy casks, which are known for their fruitiness and woody aroma. The distillery describes a sweet fragrance reminiscent of apricot and jasmine, together with a slightly bitter richness. The other, "Sherry note", is aged for nine years in sherry casks and is characterised by a sweet aroma and depth reminiscent of raisins and dried fruit. Both come in a 375 ml gift box and have an alcohol content of 35%. Under Japanese liquor classifications they fall into the category of "liqueur". The release was announced for around August to September 2026, and sales were limited to retailers in Okinawa Prefecture and the distillery's official online shop.

## How to enjoy it in Europe

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/29349033/pexels-photo-29349033.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Cask-aged spirit served neat, with a water chaser" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Cask-aged spirit served neat, with a water chaser — Photo: Andrew Patrick Photo (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

With only 1,000 bottles of each of the two limited editions, you are unlikely to come across them in Europe. Even so, the direction of finishing awamori in casks is worth remembering. If you find cask-aged awamori in a specialist sake and shochu shop in Europe, one idea is to try it neat or with a little water and compare it with whisky. Behind the aroma of the cask, you may notice the soft sweetness that comes from rice and black koji. It also pairs easily with dried fruit and dark chocolate, so it may feel familiar to European drinkers who already know sherry and brandy.
