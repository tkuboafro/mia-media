Zojirushi Corporation, a home-appliance maker headquartered in Osaka, is also known in Europe for its rice cookers and insulated bottles. The company has launched "Zo no Mai", a craft beer brewed from rice left over from rice-cooker development. According to the Niigata Nippo, it reached its sales plan of 100,000 bottles a year about six months after launch. It is a case where an effort to cut food waste has turned directly into a new alcoholic drink.

To develop a rice cooker, you have to actually cook rice and check differences in texture, aroma and how it turns out. According to reports, the rice Zojirushi cooks for such tastings amounts to around 30 tonnes a year. The work is essential to protecting product quality, but some of the rice inevitably goes uneaten. "Zo no Mai" puts that leftover rice to use as a brewing ingredient.

## What it means to brew beer from rice

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/5538159/pexels-photo-5538159.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Brewing beer. Rice and other grains are sometimes added to the malt" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Brewing beer. Rice and other grains are sometimes added to the malt — Photo: cottonbro studio (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

Beer is generally made from malted barley, but adding rice as an adjunct is not unusual. Rice is said to make a beer lighter and crisper to drink. Sake is also made from rice, but whereas sake turns the rice itself into sugar using koji (a mould grown on rice) before fermenting it, beer gets its sugars from the power of malt. The same rice, in other words, becomes a completely different drink depending on where it ends up.

That a home-appliance maker's beer met its plan in such a short time suggests that consumers care about the theme of reducing food waste. In Japan's alcoholic-drinks industry, products made from materials that tend to be thrown away, such as sake lees and non-standard fruit, are becoming more common. "Zo no Mai" is an example of a company from outside the world of drinks-making joining that trend.

At present there is no information about exports of "Zo no Mai" to Europe. Even so, the appeal of the story can be enjoyed closer to home. If you come across a beer made with rice, it may be interesting to notice how its lightness and aftertaste differ from a malt-only beer. In Europe, too, upcycled beers made from leftover bread and similar ingredients are on the rise. Setting a sake beside a rice beer and reflecting on the range of flavours that the same grain can produce is also a pleasure.
