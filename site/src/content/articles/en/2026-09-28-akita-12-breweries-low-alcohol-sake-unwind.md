Sake generally has an alcohol content of around 15% ABV. Among Japanese breweries, a movement to lower that level slightly is spreading. In Akita Prefecture, a sake-producing region on the Sea of Japan coast in northern Honshu, the liquor wholesaler Akita Ken Shurui Oroshi (Akita Ken Shurui Oroshi) worked with 12 breweries in the prefecture to plan 12 low-alcohol sakes, which went on sale on 27 July 2026.

## A limited series from 12 breweries with reduced alcohol: "Unwind"

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d5/Sugidama_%28cedar_ball%29_outside_sake_brewery%2C_Takayama%2C_May_2017.jpg" alt="A sake brewery (illustrative image)" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A sake brewery (illustrative image) — Photo: Celuici (wikimedia, CC BY-SA)</figcaption></figure>

The product is called "Bishu Okoku Akita Bin 2026 Unwind". Each participating brewery made one sake, and every one is limited to 600 bottles. All labels share the same design, with only the colour changing from brewery to brewery so they can be told apart. The alcohol content ranges from 11 to 14.5% ABV, lower than typical sake. The sakes are said to go well with meals, since they are soft on the palate and their aromas do not compete with food. The name Unwind, which means to relax in English, also suggests the aim of having people enjoy them casually at the dinner table.

## Why is the alcohol content of sake falling?

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1664477407933-dd42ed0c6c62?crop=entropy&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;fit=max&amp;fm=jpg&amp;ixid=M3wxMDg1MTk5fDB8MXxzZWFyY2h8M3x8c2FrZSUyMGJvdHRsZSUyMHBvdXJpbmclMjBjdXB8ZW58MXwwfHx8MTc5MTk0NDU0NXww&amp;ixlib=rb-4.1.0&amp;q=80&amp;w=1080" alt="Sake being poured into a masu box or a glass" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Sake being poured into a masu box or a glass — Photo: <a href="https://unsplash.com/@dropfastcollective?utm_source=the_sake_wire&amp;utm_medium=referral" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Frederick Shaw</a> (<a href="https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=the_sake_wire&amp;utm_medium=referral" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Unsplash</a>)</figcaption></figure>

Sake is generally made by adding water after pressing to adjust the alcohol content. The average level of 15 to 16% ABV has become established over a long history. According to the Japanese sake media outlet SAKE Street, the global drinks industry is moving towards lower alcohol, driven by growing health awareness, especially among younger people, and tighter regulation of drinking. In sake too, major producers have been releasing products below 10% ABV one after another, and overseas, "about the same strength as wine" is said to be one benchmark. On the other hand, because alcohol helps suppress the growth of unwanted microbes, it has also been pointed out that lower-strength sake requires greater care in quality control during production and distribution. The 12 sakes in this release are not lowered as far as below 10% ABV, and fall within a range close to that of many wines.

## How to enjoy them at the European table

With only 600 bottles of each, it is unclear whether this series will reach Europe in any quantity. However, the trend towards lower-strength sake is expected to continue. When choosing sake in a shop, try checking the alcohol content (alc.) shown on the label. At 11 to 14% ABV, you can chill it and pour it into a wine glass just as you would wine, and it should pair easily with fish dishes, vegetable dishes and mild cheeses. Lower-strength sake is delicate, so it is best kept in the refrigerator after purchase and finished soon after opening. Asking your regular sake shop or importer, "Do you have any low-alcohol sake?" is also a quick way to find a new bottle.
