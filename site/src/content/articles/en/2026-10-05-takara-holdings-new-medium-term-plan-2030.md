## Rebuilding after four straight years of falling operating profit

On 13 May 2026, Kyoto-headquartered Takara Holdings (Takara HD) announced a five-year management plan, the "Takara Group Mid-Term Management Plan 2030". Listed companies in Japan publish their management targets every few years as a "mid-term management plan". The final year of this plan is fiscal 2030 (the year ending March 2031). The numerical targets are sales of at least 493 billion yen and operating profit of at least 37.8 billion yen. The company also set targets of at least 7% for return on invested capital (ROIC) and at least 10% for return on equity (ROE).

The plan follows a harsh outcome from the previous one. Under the "Mid-Term Management Plan 2025", which ran to fiscal 2025, the company invested heavily in its overseas Japanese food wholesale business and its biotech business, both positioned as growth areas. Results, however, fell far short of the plan, and operating profit declined year on year for four consecutive years. The new plan can be seen as a set of targets intended to reverse that trend.

## A scale apart, even among the "majors"

Most sake breweries in Japan are small and medium-sized enterprises. Under Japan's Small and Medium-sized Enterprise Basic Act, a small or medium-sized sake maker is defined as one with "capital of 300 million yen or less, or 300 or fewer employees". Takara HD is a listed company with capital of about 13.2 billion yen, far above this threshold. It is also one of the breweries with annual shipments exceeding 3,000 kilolitres. At that size, Japanese rules do not allow a company to obtain a mail-order licence, and it is treated as a major, nationally distributed brand. Together, the shipments of such major producers account for 64.2% of the Japanese total. Takara HD's management policy is therefore that of a company whose scale can influence the distribution of Japanese alcohol as a whole.

The focus of investment in the previous plan was the overseas Japanese food wholesale business. This business delivers Japanese ingredients to restaurants and retailers abroad, and it has a distribution network in Europe as well. What was disclosed at the time of the announcement was mainly financial targets; it did not set out how the overseas wholesale business will be rebuilt. Further details will be worth watching.

For European readers, this news is also close to home. Some of the Japanese sake and seasonings on the shelves of your local Japanese or Asian grocery may well have arrived through the wholesale networks of such major companies. Sake from major makers tends to be consistent in quality and easy to find, which makes it a useful reference point for the standard style of sake. Tasting it alongside sake from small breweries makes the differences in style easier to see. Whether the majors continue to invest in overseas distribution may affect the variety and volume of Japanese alcohol that will line European shelves in future.
