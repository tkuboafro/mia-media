Akita Prefecture, on the Sea of Japan side of Honshu in north-eastern Japan, is well known as a sake-producing region. In the old town of its capital, Akita city, in the Omachi district, stands a craft brewery called Akita Akura Beer. On 20 May 2026, the brewery released a limited-quantity beer made with honey gathered from acacia flowers in Kosaka, a small town in the north of the prefecture. It is part of a series that turns local ingredients into beer one at a time, and the way the honey is used is distinctive.

## Why the honey goes in at the end of fermentation

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/5634205/pexels-photo-5634205.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Pale golden acacia honey" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Pale golden acacia honey — Photo: Adonyi Gábor (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

Adding honey to beer is not in itself unusual. However, because most of the sugar in honey is easily broken down by yeast, adding it early means both the sweetness and the floral aroma are easily lost during fermentation. To keep the honey's sweetness and aroma in the beer, Akita Akura Beer added it late in fermentation. Acacia honey is pale in colour, with a gentle aroma that has little of the usual strong character. The finished beer keeps its bitterness restrained and puts a mellow mouthfeel to the fore. According to the brewery, it pairs well with smoked dishes and spicy food.

## Local products, one beer at a time

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1558642452-9d2a7deb7f62?crop=entropy&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;fit=max&amp;fm=jpg&amp;ixid=M3wxMDg1MTk5fDB8MXxzZWFyY2h8MXx8YWNhY2lhJTIwaG9uZXklMjBqYXJ8ZW58MXwwfHx8MTc5MTYzNzIwMHww&amp;ixlib=rb-4.1.0&amp;q=80&amp;w=1080" alt="A jar of acacia honey" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A jar of acacia honey — Photo: <a href="https://unsplash.com/@arwinneil?utm_source=the_sake_wire&amp;utm_medium=referral" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Arwin Neil Baichoo</a> (<a href="https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=the_sake_wire&amp;utm_medium=referral" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Unsplash</a>)</figcaption></figure>

This beer is the fourth in a project that releases beers in turn, each made from the produce of a different municipality in Akita Prefecture. The brewery has set itself the goal of working with all 25 municipalities in the prefecture. Across Japan, sake breweries and craft breweries are increasingly teaming up with local farm producers to make a one-of-a-kind bottle rooted in its place. This latest effort suggests that, in beer as well as in sake, brewing that puts the origin of the ingredients front and centre is gaining ground.

## Enjoying it in Europe

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/7418943/pexels-photo-7418943.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="A pale beer served with smoked fish" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A pale beer served with smoked fish — Photo: Evgeniy Alekseyev (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

It has not been announced whether this limited beer will be sold outside Japan. Even so, the idea behind it can be tried in Europe too. When choosing a honey beer, pay attention to the stage of fermentation at which the honey was added, and the differences in how much sweetness and aroma remain will be easier to spot. For food, follow the brewery's recommended pairings and try it with smoked fish, ham or spicy dishes. A beer with gentle bitterness tends to go well with saltiness and smoky aromas. Specialist shops that stock Japanese craft beer are seeing a gradual increase in labels that use local fruit, honey, tea and the like. Reading the ingredients list on the label and looking for ingredients distinctive to their place of origin is another way to enjoy them.
