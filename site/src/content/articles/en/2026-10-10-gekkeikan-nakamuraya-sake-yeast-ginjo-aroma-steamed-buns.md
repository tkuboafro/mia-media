On 2 September 2026, the General Research Institute of Gekkeikan, a sake brewer in Fushimi, Kyoto, and Nakamuraya, a Tokyo food manufacturer, announced the results of joint research applying techniques long used to improve sake yeast to baker's yeast. The aim is to give the dough of bread and chuka-man (steamed buns with a filling) the fruity aroma, reminiscent of banana and melon, that is characteristic of ginjo sake (a premium sake fermented slowly at low temperatures).

## The technology for making "aroma yeasts" for sake

In sake brewing, many yeasts that produce large amounts of the compounds behind aroma, acidity and umami have been bred, helping to give each brand its own character. A representative ginjo aroma compound is isoamyl acetate. Bread fermentation, however, usually takes only one to four hours, far shorter than the 20 to 30 days of sake brewing, so producing a distinctive sake-like aroma through fermentation has been considered difficult.

The two companies began their joint research in 2023, starting from baker's yeast strains preserved by Nakamuraya. Following the methods used in breeding sake yeast, they selected strains using resistance to "5,5,5-trifluoroleucine", a compound similar to the amino acid leucine, as a marker, and obtained seven strains. Trial breads made with these showed increased isoamyl acetate in many of the strains, and changes in flavour were confirmed.

## The challenge of keeping the aroma after steaming

Unlike baked bread, steamed dough is known to lose its aroma easily during heating. Taking their cue from sake brewing, the research team therefore tested conditions of long fermentation at a low temperature. As a result, dough for steamed buns made with the improved yeast contained about 3.4 times as much isoamyl acetate as that made with the original yeast, and in a tasting evaluation the fruity ginjo aroma was reportedly clearly perceptible. The results were presented in August 2026 at the annual meeting of the Japanese Society for Food Science and Technology, held at Tohoku University. As of the announcement, no timing had been set for commercial products using this technology.

## Enjoying it in Europe

This research is one example of how sake yeast technology can be put to use beyond sake. To get to know the aroma first-hand, the quickest route is to try a sake labelled "Ginjo" or "Daiginjo" in a wine glass, lightly chilled. The main compound behind the banana and melon notes that rise as you swirl the glass is isoamyl acetate. Paired with steamed buns such as bao, now easier to find in European cities, you can compare at the table the "aroma created by yeast" that this research is aiming for.
