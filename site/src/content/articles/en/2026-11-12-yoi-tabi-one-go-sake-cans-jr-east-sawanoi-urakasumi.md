## Local sake by the “ichigo” at station convenience stores

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://prcdn.freetls.fastly.net/release_image/34286/392/34286-392-a759b13b347e9b64eca34928e447c74d-412x284.png?width=1200&amp;height=900" alt="The “Yoi Tabi.” series of single-serve cans, designed after railway carriages" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>The “Yoi Tabi.” series of single-serve cans, designed after railway carriages — Image: PR TIMES（JR東日本スタートアップ） (press release)</figcaption></figure>

JR East Cross Station Retail Company, which runs the NewDays station convenience stores for the JR East Group, and Agnavi, a Kanagawa-based start-up that makes single-serve sake cans (“ichigo-can”), will roll out a new series called “Yoi Tabi.” (“A Good Journey.”) in stages from 17 November 2026. Through a capital and business alliance with JR East Startup, the two companies have already used these cans to promote the appeal of regional Japan. This time, the aim is to let people casually enjoy sake from breweries along the railway lines while travelling by station or train. Each can costs 363 yen (excluding tax), and sales will end once stocks run out.

## Two breweries, two trains: Sawanoi and Urakasumi

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://prcdn.freetls.fastly.net/release_image/34286/392/34286-392-115697a307ce8af438c3424d540cde74-416x286.png?width=1200&amp;height=900" alt="Two cans featuring a Yamanote Line train and an Akita Shinkansen train" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Two cans featuring a Yamanote Line train and an Akita Shinkansen train — Image: PR TIMES（JR東日本スタートアップ） (press release)</figcaption></figure>

The first release features two brands. One is “Sawanoi” from Ozawa Shuzo in Ome, in western Tokyo. A crisp, classic sake brewed with spring water from Okutama, it carries an illustration of the E235 series train on the Yamanote Line, which circles central Tokyo. It can be enjoyed at different temperatures, from chilled to hot, and is said to suit Japanese food in general. The other is “Urakasumi” from Saura in Shiogama, a port town in Miyagi Prefecture in north-eastern Honshu. Made only with rice grown in Miyagi, it is a food-friendly sake with a gentle aroma, a soft mouthfeel and a clean finish. Its can shows the E6 series train of the Akita Shinkansen “Komachi”, which links Tokyo and Akita. It is recommended chilled up to “nurukan” (warmed to a little above body temperature).

## What the 180 mL size means

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://prcdn.freetls.fastly.net/release_image/34286/392/34286-392-d19f7d579c84dd980aea8b3b4692ac39-498x738.png?width=1200&amp;height=900" alt="A single-serve sake can in an easy-to-hold size" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A single-serve sake can in an easy-to-hold size — Image: PR TIMES（JR東日本スタートアップ） (press release)</figcaption></figure>

An “ichigo” is the traditional Japanese unit for drinking sake and equals 180 mL, an amount that is easy for one person to finish. Agnavi makes and sells “ichigo-cans” as its own brand in Japan and abroad, citing as strengths that they hold just the right amount, are light, are easy to carry and have a small environmental footprint. For sake, where 720 mL and 1.8 L bottles are the norm, a can that lets people try a small amount with ease is one way to reach new drinkers.

“Yoi Tabi.” is a product for NewDays stores in Japan, but for European readers the format is what deserves attention. If you come across cans or small bottles of around 180 mL in a shop that sells sake, you could check the brewery's location on the label, try it chilled first, then warm it in a water bath to around 40°C and compare. You can easily see, with only a small amount, how temperature changes the aroma and mouthfeel. It is also a format that should be easy to take along on a picnic or a train journey.
