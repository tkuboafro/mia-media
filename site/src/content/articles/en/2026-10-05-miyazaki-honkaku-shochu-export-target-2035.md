In Miyazaki Prefecture in southern Kyushu, a leading production area for the Japanese spirit honkaku shochu, a plan to expand exports sharply is set to receive national support. Miyazaki's "Local Industry Growth Plan" for the alcoholic drinks sector has been incorporated this fiscal year into the "Regional Future Strategy", a national policy to revitalise regional economies. The prefecture's effort to sell its shochu to overseas markets has, in effect, won formal recognition from the government.

## Aiming for roughly five times the export value by 2035

The plan sets out concrete numerical targets. Honkaku shochu exports, which stood at ¥288 million in 2024, are to rise to about ¥750 million in 2030 and about ¥1.5 billion in 2035. That would mean growth of roughly 2.6 times the 2024 figure by 2030 and about five times by 2035. The watchword is to make honkaku shochu a "world-class premium spirit". Local stakeholders intend to pool their efforts and step up promotion overseas still further.

## What is honkaku shochu?

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/2889366/pexels-photo-2889366.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Harvesting sweet potatoes, a raw material for shochu" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Harvesting sweet potatoes, a raw material for shochu — Photo: Mark Stebnicki (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

Shochu is one of Japan's best-known drinks alongside sake, but it is still little known in Europe. "Honkaku shochu" is shochu made by distilling just once in a pot still, which leaves the aroma and flavour of the raw ingredients clearly intact. The ingredients vary widely, from sweet potato, barley and rice to buckwheat, and, as with whisky or brandy, the character changes greatly depending on the ingredient and the maker. The typical strength is around 25% ABV, which places it lighter than whisky and stronger than wine.

## How to enjoy it in Europe

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/28180094/pexels-photo-28180094.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Spirit poured into a rocks glass at a European-style bar counter" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Spirit poured into a rocks glass at a European-style bar counter — Photo: Nano Erdozain (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

The plan suggests that Miyazaki shochu may appear more and more often in European liquor shops and bars. If you spot a bottle, check the label for the ingredient. Sweet potato shochu tends to have a sweet, floral aroma, while barley shochu is often toasty and light, and the difference is easy to notice when you taste them side by side. The standard ways to drink it are on the rocks, diluted with cold water, or mixed with hot water. Hot water opens up the aroma all at once, which suits a cold European winter. Because the strength is moderate and it does not overpower food, it also goes well with grilled meat or fish, and with cheese.
