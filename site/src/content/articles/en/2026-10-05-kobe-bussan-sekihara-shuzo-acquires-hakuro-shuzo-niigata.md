Kobe Bussan (headquartered in Kakogawa, Hyogo Prefecture), the food company that operates the Gyomu Super discount supermarket chain across Japan, announced in April 2026 that its consolidated subsidiary Sekihara Shuzo (Nagaoka, Niigata Prefecture) will acquire all shares in Hakuro Shuzo, another sake brewery in Nagaoka. The news was reported by the local newspaper Kobe Shimbun. The aim is to increase production capacity for sake, for which demand is growing. A company whose core businesses are retail and food manufacturing buys a brewery, then adds another. In the sake industry, this kind of consolidation of breweries by capital from outside the industry is becoming increasingly noticeable.

## Why a supermarket company owns sake breweries

Kobe Bussan champions what it calls an integrated food production and sales system. The idea is to handle everything from raw material production and processing to sales within its own group. In line with this policy, the company has been actively acquiring food manufacturers and other businesses, and Sekihara Shuzo joined the group as one of them. According to reports, demand for Sekihara Shuzo's sake has been rising in recent years. However, it is not easy for a brewery to increase its tanks and kurabito (the skilled craftspeople who make sake) in a short time. So the plan is to bring in Hakuro Shuzo, a neighbouring brewery that already makes and sells sake, and so put in place a set-up that can raise output. The reports do not disclose the purchase price or how Hakuro Shuzo's brands will be handled in future.

## Generational change at Nagaoka's breweries

Niigata Prefecture, on the Sea of Japan coast, is a rice-growing region and one of Japan's leading sake-producing areas. Nagaoka, in the middle of the prefecture, is home to many breweries, and in the past few years a succession of them have changed hands. According to the sake-focused media outlet SAKE Street, Aoi Shuzo, also in Nagaoka, was taken over at the end of 2024 by an entrepreneur from the financial industry. The new owner is said to be aiming for a small brewery that competes on quality rather than volume. This acquisition, by contrast, is a move to increase output using the capital of a large company. The directions are polar opposites, yet both belong to the same trend: outside people and capital taking over breweries that have no successor. As Japan's domestic market shrinks, the number of breweries is falling, and those that remain are being reorganised under new stewards.

## What European readers should note

For breweries that come under large-scale capital, investment in equipment may stabilise quality and make it easier to secure supply for export. On the other hand, brands may be streamlined and the flavour direction may change. When choosing Niigata sake in Europe, look at the "manufacturer" field on the back label. Separate from the brand name, it gives the name of the brewery that actually made the sake. Niigata sake is known for tanrei karakuchi, a clean, dry style that you never tire of drinking. By tasting sake from the same Nagaoka brewery before and after a change of ownership, you may be able to judge the industry's changes with your own palate.
