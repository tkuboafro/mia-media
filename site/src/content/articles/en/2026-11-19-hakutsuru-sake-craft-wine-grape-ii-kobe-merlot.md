Hakutsuru Sake Brewing, one of Japan’s largest sake makers, headquartered in Kobe, released “Wine Budo II” on 10 October 2026 as the 22nd instalment of its experimental small-batch series “HAKUTSURU SAKE CRAFT”. It is an unusual hybrid that combines rice, the traditional raw material of sake, with Merlot, a red wine grape variety. The Japanese trade paper Nihon Shokuryo Shimbun reports the news.

## A hybrid sake where rice meets Merlot

The Merlot used was harvested in Kobe this autumn. Because an ingredient other than rice has been added, Japan’s liquor tax law classes it not as ordinary “seishu (sake)” but as “other brewed alcoholic beverages”. At 11% ABV, it is designed to be lighter than typical sake, which is often around 15%. The colour of the fully ripened Merlot dissolves into the drink, giving it a vivid, purplish red in the glass. Its aroma has a floral, fruity elegance reminiscent of daiginjo (the top tier of sake brewing, in which the rice is polished heavily and fermented slowly at low temperature to bring out a showy fruit fragrance), layered with the sweetness and acidity of fresh grapes.

In Japan, production of wine grapes is limited, and sake breweries cannot easily get hold of them. Since 2024, Hakutsuru Sake Brewing has been the operator of the local Kobe Wine business, and the access to Kobe-grown wine grapes that this brought is the background to this product. A sake brewery that also makes wine and brings two brewing cultures together in one bottle is a good example of a new style that has been spreading in Japan in recent years.

## How to enjoy it in Europe

The series is mainly produced in small quantities, and it is not currently known whether it will be imported directly into Europe. Even so, “hybrid sake” that crosses the boundary between sake and wine is worth remembering as a new trend in Japanese brewing. If you come across “other brewed alcoholic beverages” or fruit-based sake on the lists of specialist shops or importers, do take a closer look. To taste it, serve it well chilled in a wine glass, so that you can enjoy both the colour and the aroma. Its light strength and sweet-sour character should also suit the dishes you would normally pair with a rosé, such as cured ham, goat’s cheese or starters with berries.
