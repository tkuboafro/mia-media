## Craft sake, born outside the licensing 'door'

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://prcdn.freetls.fastly.net/release_image/134435/7/134435-7-2ba5514fd57404ca88476be18cf2c5a0-1452x817.jpg?width=1200&amp;height=900" alt="A still from the documentary film Ine to Kaze" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A still from the documentary film Ine to Kaze — Image: PR TIMES（アウトクロップ） (press release)</figcaption></figure>

For roughly 80 years since the war, Japan has granted almost no new licences to produce sake (seishu). For a younger generation wanting to start brewing, the front door has effectively been shut. Craft sake was born in that context. The term refers to a new kind of drink that builds on sake-brewing techniques but is made outside the framework of the existing sake licence. Even in Japan, many people reportedly still do not know the name. The film records this movement from its earliest days through to its gradual acceptance.

## Oga, Paris, Minamisoma: three brewers

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://prcdn.freetls.fastly.net/release_image/134435/7/134435-7-26edc9787873320ab5820c2959fd00ce-2176x1224.jpg?width=1200&amp;height=900" alt="A press still from Ine to Kaze" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A press still from Ine to Kaze — Image: PR TIMES（アウトクロップ） (press release)</figcaption></figure>

The film centres on three brewers. The first is Shuhei Okazumi. On the Oga Peninsula in Akita Prefecture, facing the Sea of Japan, he has turned an old railway station building into a brewery and aims to create a sake 'special zone'. The second, Shoya Imai, has opened a brewery in Paris, France, and wants to grow sake into a 'world sake', brewed and enjoyed around the globe. The third, Taisuke Sato, combines free-spirited ideas with traditional Tohoku brewing methods in Minamisoma, Fukushima Prefecture, where the population has fallen because of the nuclear power plant accident. Facing headwinds such as falling interest in sake and a shrinking market, each of the three is shaking up tradition in their own way.

## From criticism of the system to human drama

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://prcdn.freetls.fastly.net/release_image/134435/7/134435-7-184f3b41054df829c2abb563bc4f1934-757x757.jpg?width=1200&amp;height=900" alt="The official visual for Ine to Kaze" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>The official visual for Ine to Kaze — Image: PR TIMES（アウトクロップ） (press release)</figcaption></figure>

The film is directed, shot and edited by Travis Matsumoto, who is based in Akita City. It is their first feature-length documentary. When filming began, they expected to make a work criticising the industry's system. But as they kept visiting the sites, they realised that it is people who brew the sake and people who make the rules, and so they decided to portray the story as a human drama that stays close to the brewers' point of view. Through interviews with government bodies and industry associations, they say they also came to see that there is too little discussion about the future of a traditional industry, and that young brewers and those setting the rules are not on the same wavelength. The running time is 99 minutes and the language is Japanese. It will have a preview screening in Akita on 20 February 2027, and will then be released progressively across Japan from 27 February, starting at Eurospace in Tokyo.

No screenings in Europe have been announced at present. Further information can be found on the official website (inetokaze.com) and the official Instagram account.
