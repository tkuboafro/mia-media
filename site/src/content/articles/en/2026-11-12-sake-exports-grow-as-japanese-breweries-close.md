## Exports up 1.7 times in five years, reaching 81 countries and regions

According to the Japanese business magazine Keizaikai, domestic shipments of sake have fallen steadily since their peak in the 1970s and are now less than a quarter of that level. Sake's share of all alcoholic drinks has also dropped from about 30% to around 5%. Of the roughly 4,000 breweries that once operated across Japan, only about 1,100 are actually active. Recently, around three breweries a month are said to be forced into bankruptcy or closure.

Overseas, meanwhile, has become the fastest-growing area in the industry. Exports in 2025 were worth about 45.9 billion yen, up roughly 6% on the previous year, with volume at about 33,549 kilolitres, up roughly 8%. According to statistics from Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, export value has grown 1.7 times over the past five years. Sake now reaches 81 countries and regions, with China, the United States, Hong Kong and South Korea leading by value. Behind this are the inscription of Japanese cuisine on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list and the worldwide spread of sushi and tempura. In addition, among the more than 40 million visitors to Japan each year, a growing number discover sake there and keep drinking it after they return home.

## From "shipped from Japan" to "brewed locally"

Overseas production has also entered a new phase. Ozeki, Takara Shuzo and Gekkeikan began shipping in the United States between 1979 and 1989, but their bases were all in California, home to many people of Japanese descent, and their main customers were Japanese supermarkets and Japanese restaurants. By contrast, Dassai of Yamaguchi Prefecture invested about 8.5 billion yen in 2023 to build a brewery in New York State and launched "DASSAI BLUE" aimed at ordinary American consumers from the outset. In the same year, Hakkaisan Brewery of Niigata Prefecture also set up a brewery in New York in a joint venture with local capital. The article sees Europe, where Japan is even more popular than in the United States, as another promising market.

## Quality over quantity: a new high-price trend at home

There are changes at home, too. Shipment volume is falling, but shipment value is roughly flat. High-value products, such as ginjo sake, which is brewed from rice polished away to a greater degree to give a fragrant finish, are pushing up the unit price. Bottles priced above 100,000 yen have appeared, and a long-aged sake brewed in 1971 by Shiraki Kosuke Shoten of Gifu Prefecture sells for 550,000 yen. In Iwakuni, Yamaguchi Prefecture, Dassai's home town, the company is building a third brewery devoted solely to high-priced products, due to start operating in 2028. Sake is shifting from an everyday drink competing on low prices to a business that sells small volumes at high prices.

If you are enjoying sake in Europe, we recommend trying it in settings other than Japanese restaurants. Sake goes especially well with seafood, and can be enjoyed chilled, warmed or sparkling. Try pairing it with familiar dishes such as mussels or grilled white fish. "Junmai daiginjo" on a label refers to a top grade brewed without added brewing alcohol, using rice polished until more than half of the outer layer is removed. Sake brewed outside Japan is also a new category worth watching from now on.
