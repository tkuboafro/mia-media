According to the Japan Sake and Shochu Makers Association (Nihon Shuzo Kumiai Chuokai), sake exports to the US in 2025 were worth about 11.1 billion yen, and the US remained the world's largest market by export volume. In November 2026, the Japanese international logistics and customs clearance company Pegasus Global Express published an explanatory article setting out what breweries and trading companies tackling this market should sort out first. The key point is that "shipping" sake to the US and "selling" sake in the US are entirely different matters.

## Three categories and the "three-tier system"

The article divides sake bound for the US into three categories. The first is what "can be sent as it is": small quantities sent by international courier as samples or gifts. These can be shipped if the recipient, the import conditions and the carrier's rules are all satisfied. The second is what "can be sent with conditions", which covers products sold on a continuing basis. The third is what "cannot be sent": alcoholic drinks may not be sent by international Express Mail Service (EMS) through the post, whether for commercial or personal use.

To sell continuously, you need to understand the US "three-tier system". Japanese breweries and trading companies first sell to a US importer; the importer supplies wholesalers in each state; and the wholesalers deliver to restaurants and liquor shops. In principle, a brewery cannot deliver directly to a local restaurant. Because only a handful of importers can cover every US state, the article says it is realistic to start with California or New York, where demand for Japanese food is high.

## Sake: both beer and wine

The US system has no separate category for "sake". Federal tax law names sake explicitly in its definition of "beer", yet federal alcohol law defines drinks of 7% alcohol by volume or more as "wine", so labelling is treated in the same way as for wine. As a result, an importer must hold an importer's permit from the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB) and obtain prior label approval (COLA) for each brand. The review checks the product name, alcohol content, place of origin, volume, health warning statement, list of ingredients and so on. In addition, a brewery's production and storage facilities must be registered with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), with the registration renewed every even-numbered year. On the Japanese side too, a trading company that exports other companies' sake generally needs an export alcohol wholesaling licence issued by the tax office.

## Tips for choosing sake in Europe

Sake on the shelves of European shops also reaches them through importers. The US example shows that the alcohol content, ingredients and place of origin on a bottle's label are information that must match each country's review and paperwork. When you find a brand that interests you, it is worth checking the name of the importer. Looking for other breweries handled by the same importer can sometimes help you find sake to your taste. Also, just as junmai (sake made only from rice, rice koji and water) and ginjo (sake brewed from rice polished from the outside to 60% or less of its original weight) are approved and distributed brand by brand in the US, in the export trade differences in style are treated as differences in product. Comparing bottles by their grade labels can sometimes reveal each brewery's individual character.
