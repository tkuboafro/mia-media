Suntory Holdings has announced that its honorary chief blender, Seiichi Koshimizu, has received the Agency for Cultural Affairs' "Culinary Treasure" (Shoku no Shiho) award for fiscal 2026 (Reiwa 8). In this inaugural round of the honour, Koshimizu was recognised for his work on the single malts Yamazaki and Hakushu and the blended whisky Hibiki. One of the makers involved in the process by which Japanese whisky became known around the world has thus been recognised in the setting of the nation's cultural administration.

## A new honour launched by the Agency for Cultural Affairs

The Agency for Cultural Affairs is the government body responsible for Japan's culture, the arts and cultural properties. This fiscal year it has launched the "Culinary Treasure" honour for the first time, and a whisky maker has been chosen as one of its first recipients. In Japan, alcohol production has long been discussed mainly as an industry; this award can be seen as one example of how the skill and sensibility involved in making distilled spirits are increasingly being treated as part of food culture.

## The blender's work behind Yamazaki, Hakushu and Hibiki

A blender is a craftsperson who tastes and tells apart a great many whiskies from different distilleries, cask types and ageing periods, then combines them to create the flavour of a single product and to keep it consistent over time. Yamazaki and Hakushu are single malts named after their respective distilleries, while Hibiki is a blended whisky made by layering multiple whiskies. Koshimizu has been involved with all three of these brands, each with a distinct character. The task is to keep the flavour of the same brand unchanged year after year while also exploring new forms of expression. The significance of this award lies in the fact that this inconspicuous work has been brought into the light in the form of a national honour.

## Enjoying them in Europe

Yamazaki, Hakushu and Hibiki can sometimes be found in specialist whisky shops and bars in Europe too. If you have the chance to compare a single malt with a blend, the differences may be easier to notice. Start by checking the aroma neat, then add a few drops of water; the aroma can open up and the impression can change. A single malt lets you enjoy the character of one whisky, while a blend lets you enjoy the harmony of many. Noticing that difference is one way to get closer to the work of a blender.
