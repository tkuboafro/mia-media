Kirishima Shuzo, a shochu maker based in Miyazaki Prefecture, is launching its first-ever craft gin, AKAKIRI GIN. It is an overseas-only product that will not be sold in Japan, and it is due to go on sale gradually in more than 20 countries, starting with the United States. According to the local broadcaster Miyazaki Broadcasting, it is a Miyazaki-born “local gin” that combines the skills the company has built up making shochu with ingredients from Kyushu.

## Why a shochu distillery is making gin

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/28792576/pexels-photo-28792576.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="A traditional pot still of the kind also used in shochu distilleries" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A traditional pot still of the kind also used in shochu distilleries — Photo: Tiago Antonio (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

Shochu is a traditional Japanese distilled spirit made from ingredients such as rice, barley and sweet potato. Koji, a type of mould, turns the starch in the raw ingredients into sugar, which is then fermented and distilled. Miyazaki Prefecture lies in southern Kyushu and is known as a region where shochu making thrives. Gin is also a distilled spirit, and it gets its character from botanicals, plant materials used for flavouring, such as juniper berries. For a shochu distillery with distilling expertise, gin is a field close to its original business. The reports note that shipments of craft gin have grown more than fourfold, and they cite as an attraction that using local ingredients gives each region its own character.

## The aim: a gateway to shochu

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/3039671/pexels-photo-3039671.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="A gin and tonic served at a bar counter" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A gin and tonic served at a bar counter — Photo: Dimitris Binioris (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

Hana Tanaka of Kirishima Shuzo’s product development section says she hopes this gin will introduce people overseas to shochu. The goal is a product that is stocked in bars abroad and enjoyed there. Shochu is an everyday drink in Japan, but overseas it is less well known than sake or whisky. The export strategy appears to be to use gin, which is familiar in bars in many countries, as an entry point: get people to taste it first, and then spark an interest in shochu too.

## How to find and enjoy it in Europe

Which European countries are among the 20-plus markets has not yet been announced. Readers in Europe would do well to keep an eye on new-product news from bars and specialist shops that stock Japanese spirits. If you find it, first try a small amount neat to see what aromas the Kyushu ingredients give it. Then mix it with tonic water, which makes it easy to compare with the gin you usually drink. You could also try a genuine honkaku shochu, available in Europe too, on the rocks or diluted with water, which may let you taste the distilling tradition that underpins this gin.
