Three sakes from a single small brewery in Matsuyama, on the island of Shikoku, came home from Hong Kong with medals this month. Minakuchi Shuzo, founded in 1895 and the only brewery left in the hot-spring district of Dōgo, took a silver and two bronzes at the Oriental Sake Awards 2026 for its Nikitatsu range – and its everyday junmai was named one of the top three sakes for a plate of Cantonese stir-fried river shrimp. For a house that had already collected gold at the Milano Sake Challenge, a sixth place in the brewery ranking at Kura Master in France and a medal for every label at the International Wine Challenge this year, it rounds off a remarkable season.

## Why a Hong Kong competition matters

Europeans tend to hear about the big Western sake competitions – the IWC in London, Kura Master in Paris. The Oriental Sake Awards, organised by Hong Kong’s sake industry association, are the first international-standard sake competition held in Asia outside Japan, and they carry weight for a simple reason: according to Japan’s National Tax Agency, about 60 per cent of the country’s sake export value goes to the major Asian markets. Judging is done blind by Asian professionals – buyers, sommeliers, brewers – and OSA is the only competition in Asia outside Japan whose results count towards the World Sakagura Ranking.

Since 2024 the awards have also run a side event called the Perfect Match Challenge. This year, working with the Hong Kong Cantonese restaurant Kin’s Kitchen, the organisers set four dishes; local importers put forward sakes for each, and the judges picked a top three per plate. Nikitatsu Junmai made the cut for stir-fried river shrimp with Indian almonds.

## The three medal winners

The silver went to NIKITATSU2025, a junmai daiginjo brewed for the brewery’s 130th anniversary. Its Yamada Nishiki rice was grown by young farmers in Kumakōgen in the Ehime highlands and polished to 45 per cent; the label nods to Dōgo’s legend of a white heron said to have healed its wounds in the spring. At 14 per cent alcohol it is a gentle, rice-sweet sake, judged in the ‘rich and aromatic’ ginjo category.

NIKITATSU2024, the first in the series, marked the full reopening of the Dōgo Onsen Honkan bathhouse in 2024. It is made with the local Shizuku-hime rice polished all the way down to 30 per cent, brewed with Dōgo’s own spring water, and shows a fresh, apple-like aroma with a clean finish. It took bronze in the ‘light and clean’ group, a year after winning Best of Ehime at the same awards.

The third medal, a bronze, went to Nikitatsu Junmai Ginjo Sakurahime Yeast. The yeast was isolated from the flower of Sakurahime, a delphinium bred in Ehime, and it gives the sake a floral nose with juicy fruit – the brewery suggests cream stews and steak, which will sound familiar at a European dinner table.

## A brewery that tears its own curtain

Minakuchi’s founding family hands down a saying: ‘Don’t protect the noren – tear it.’ The noren is the cloth curtain hanging at a shop’s entrance, shorthand for a business’s good name. The house has taken the motto literally, brewing with local farmers, experimenting with new yeasts and making Dōgo Beer alongside its sake, under the slogan ‘from Dōgo to the world, from the world to Dōgo’.

For readers in Europe, the useful lesson lies in the grades. ‘Junmai’ means only rice, kōji and water went in; ‘ginjo’ and ‘daiginjo’ mean the rice was polished to at most 60 and 50 per cent of its original size, and the lower the number, the more delicate and aromatic the sake tends to be. If you find a junmai daiginjo, serve it cool, around 10–12 °C, in a white-wine glass, and look for apple or pear on the nose. Save the plain junmai for the table: try it slightly warm with a shrimp stir-fry or anything with garlic and soy, and notice how the rice flavour lifts the dish rather than competing with it.
