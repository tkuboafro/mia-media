The National Tax Agency, which oversees alcohol administration in Japan, has been accepting applications for the "FY2026 (Reiwa 8) Alcohol Industry Promotion Support Subsidy", designed to help sake breweries and other alcohol producers expand overseas. J-Net21, an information site supporting small and medium-sized enterprises, reported on it on 19 August. The deadline for the 4th round was 9 September and for the 5th round 7 October; both have now passed. Projects addressing rising sake rice prices and US tariff measures were to be given priority in selection. It is a scheme that shows clearly which problems the government regards as most pressing.

## Up to ¥10 million for overseas product development and promotion

The subsidy had two categories. The main one, the "Overseas Expansion Support Category", covers efforts to expand exports and add value to products. Examples include developing new products tailored to overseas tastes and promotional activities to raise awareness of Japanese sake. It also covers preparing breweries for visitors and planning brewery tours across a region. The ceiling is ¥10 million per project. When several businesses join forces, the ceiling rises with the number of partners, reaching ¥15 million for six or more. The government covers half of eligible costs.

## With sake rice prices and US tariffs in view

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/36047049/pexels-photo-36047049.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Close-up of rice grains, the raw material for sake brewing" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Close-up of rice grains, the raw material for sake brewing — Photo: Mustafa Akın (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

The other category, the "New Market Development Support Category", backs management reform and structural change in the industry. It supports product differentiation, diversification of sales methods and the use of ICT (information and communications technology) to make production and distribution more efficient, with up to ¥5 million available. The subsidy rate is one half or two thirds, depending on the number of employees.

The conditions for priority selection deserve attention. Priority goes to plans that tackle two headwinds: the rising price of rice suited to sake brewing (sake rice) and US tariff measures. In effect, the government sees raw material costs and the trade environment in overseas markets as problems facing the whole industry. If efforts to reduce dependence on any one market spread, attention could well turn to Europe.

## What European readers may want to watch

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/37849772/pexels-photo-37849772.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Rice variety and polishing ratio printed on a label" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Rice variety and polishing ratio printed on a label — Photo: Iban Lopez Luna (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

The results of the subsidy will not appear on shop shelves straight away. Even so, we may see more new products designed with European tastes in mind, and more brewery introductions in several languages. If you come across an unfamiliar brand at an importer or specialist shop, ask whether it was made for overseas markets. Rising sake rice prices can show up not only in the price but also in the choice of rice and in revised polishing ratios (the proportion showing how far the surface of the rice has been milled away). Looking at the rice variety and polishing ratio on the label can reveal how brewers are adapting to a tough situation.
