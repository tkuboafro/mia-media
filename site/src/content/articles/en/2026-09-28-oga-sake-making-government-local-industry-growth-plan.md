## What has been certified

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/25484685/pexels-photo-25484685.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="Japanese countryside (illustrative image)" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Japanese countryside (illustrative image) — Photo: Huu Huynh (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

On 28 August 2026, the Japanese government announced that it had certified sake brewing in Oga, Akita Prefecture, under the Local Industry Growth Plan, part of the "Regional Future Strategy", a policy designed to revitalise local economies. Oga is a small city in Akita Prefecture in northern Honshu, located on a peninsula that juts out into the Sea of Japan. What was recognised is an effort in which the city and its local breweries join forces to open up new markets while making the most of local resources. This is the first time a municipality in the Tohoku region has received certification under the plan.

## Industrial policy built on three tiers: nation, prefecture and city

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/8896605/pexels-photo-8896605.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="A town in rural Japan known for sake brewing" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A town in rural Japan known for sake brewing — Photo: Nguyen Hung (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

The Regional Future Strategy consists of three plans: the Strategic Industry Cluster Plan, led by the national government; the Regional Industry Cluster Plan, drawn up by prefectures; and the Local Industry Growth Plan, formulated by municipalities themselves. Certification brings financial support from the national government. It can be seen as a mechanism through which national support reaches not only big cities and large companies but also small municipalities and the industries they have chosen for themselves, in this case sake brewing. However, the amount of support, the specific projects and the names of the breweries involved have not been disclosed in the publicly available part of the reporting.

## What it means for a community to bring small producers together

For a long time, virtually no new licences to produce sake (seishu) for the domestic market were issued in Japan. In recent years, small breweries across the country have therefore been taking up a new style known as "craft sake": made from rice but with fruit, herbs and other ingredients added, and brewed under a different category of liquor licence from seishu. The certification can be read as one example of a new trend in which a local authority brings the activities of such small producers together and the national government backs them. It is worth noting that not only long-established breweries but also new producers are starting to be treated as bearers of the local industry.

For European readers who want to enjoy this trend, the easiest route is to look for rice-based drinks sold as "craft sake" in specialist sake shops and online stores. Many are lighter than seishu, with more pronounced fruit and herb aromas. Serve them well chilled in a white wine glass, and pair them with seafood starters or fresh cheese to bring out their character. It will also be worth watching how Oga's plan eventually translates into exports and products for overseas markets.
