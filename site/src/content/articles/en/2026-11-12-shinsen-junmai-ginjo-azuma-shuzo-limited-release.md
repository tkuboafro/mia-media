Some of Japan's local sakes can hardly be found outside their home area. Shinsen, brewed by Azuma Shuzo in Komatsu, Ishikawa Prefecture, is one of them. Ishikawa lies on the Sea of Japan coast, roughly in the middle of Honshu. The brewery has made a limited edition, "Shinsen Junmai Ginjo Umakuchi Nomu Silica Jikomi", using natural water from Kirishima in southern Kyushu for the brewing. Its seller, Qvou Co., Ltd., has put it on early sale on its official website from 6 November 2026. Total production is 3,000 bottles, and there are currently no plans for a re-release.

## A brewery devoted to its home town since 1860

Azuma Shuzo was founded in 1860. It brews in a stone-built brewery that is a nationally registered tangible cultural property. Guided by the idea that its sake should be "enjoyed in its best condition in Komatsu, our home town", the brewery has as a rule declined to ship outside Ishikawa Prefecture. For that reason Shinsen has been called a "phantom local sake" even within Japan. This bottle offers one of the few chances to taste Shinsen outside the prefecture.

## Brewed with Kyushu spring water, and a gold medal in France

Around 80% of sake is water. For this batch, the brewing water was Kirishima's natural water "Nomu Silica". According to the brewers, fermentation went better than expected, and the smoothness on the palate was completely different from their usual sake. The finished sake won a gold medal at the Concours Mondial des Féminalise 2025 (Féminalise World Wine Competition) in France.

Junmai ginjo is one of the tokutei meisho-shu (the premium classifications under Japan's liquor tax law), made only from rice, rice koji and water. The rice polishing ratio is 60%, meaning the outer 40% of each grain is milled away to reduce the sources of off-flavours. The rice used is 74% Gohyakumangoku, 20% Yamada Nishiki and 6% Hiyokumochi. The yeast is Ishikawa's Kanazawa yeast, and the alcohol content is kept slightly low at 14.5%.

## Flavour and food pairing

True to the name "umakuchi", savoury depth and sweetness spread softly and then fade away cleanly, leaving no bitterness. You can pour it into a red wine glass to let the aroma open up, or warm it gently to around 42 degrees to bring out more of the savoury richness. The brewery recommends pairing it with sweet-savoury dishes such as kabayaki eel or sukiyaki, as well as cheese and cheesecake.

Obtaining this sake in Europe is difficult, because the early sale is limited to the official website and telephone orders within Japan. The price is 4,500 yen including tax for 720 ml (list price 5,940 yen). Still, there are lessons to take from this story: a brewery that does not ship outside its prefecture has stepped out on the back of international recognition, and the brewing water can change the flavour considerably. When choosing sake, check the label or the importer's information for the rice, polishing ratio and yeast, and for details of the brewing water too. If you can get hold of an umakuchi junmai ginjo, try comparing it in a red wine glass and served warm, and pair it with teriyaki or mature cheese.
