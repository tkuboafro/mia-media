## Fifteen Japanese companies exhibit at the Berlin bar trade fair

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/16859958/pexels-photo-16859958.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="A bar counter scene of the kind recreated at the Berlin fair" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A bar counter scene of the kind recreated at the Berlin fair — Photo: Skills Media (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

Bar Convent Berlin (BCB) 2026, an international trade fair for the bar industry, took place in Berlin, Germany, from 12 to 14 October 2026. Marking its 20th anniversary, this year's event drew distillers, importers and bar professionals from 94 countries, with around 11,000 visitors attending. From Japan, the National Tax Agency, which oversees alcoholic beverages, ran the Japan Pavilion, where 15 companies and organisations exhibited. The pavilion was designed to convey the breadth of Japanese drinks, from sake (seishu) and shochu, Japan's traditional distilled spirit, to whisky, gin and liqueurs. Exhibitors also showed ingenuity in their presentation, including booths styled like bars.

## Koji as a new cocktail ingredient

The pavilion's other theme was Japan's fermentation culture. A workshop titled "Japanese Tradition in Modern Cocktails" centred on koji, with the aim of deepening understanding not only of sake and shochu but also of fermentation techniques and the raw ingredients themselves. Koji is steamed rice or other grains on which koji mould (a type of fungus) has been grown. It converts starch into sugar and forms the foundation of sake, shochu, miso and soy sauce. In recent years, interest in fermentation and umami has been growing in Europe's bar industry, and koji is attracting attention as an ingredient that creates new flavours. According to JETRO, Japanese fermentation culture was one of the industry's key topics at this year's BCB.

## A changing European market, and how to enjoy it at the bar

<figure class="embed embed--image"><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/4667179/pexels-photo-4667179.jpeg?auto=compress&amp;cs=tinysrgb&amp;dpr=2&amp;h=650&amp;w=940" alt="A light drink mixed with ice and soda" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A light drink mixed with ice and soda — Photo: cottonbro studio (Pexels)</figcaption></figure>

The European market itself is also changing. According to the German Brewers Federation (DBB), Germany's production of alcohol-free beer in 2024 reached a record of around 700 million litres, more than double the figure for 2004. In addition, from August 2026 some obligations under the EU's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) apply, raising questions such as how easily containers can be recycled. Japanese producers will likely be expected not only to communicate quality and individuality but also to develop products with such health-conscious trends and environmental regulations in mind.

For European readers, this trend can be felt in familiar bars too. If you spot "koji", "shochu" or "sake" on a cocktail menu, it may be worth giving it a try. It is said that starting with shochu in a simple serve with soda water makes it easier to grasp the character of each raw ingredient. With a koji cocktail, asking the bartender how the sweetness and umami come into play may reveal what makes Japanese fermentation so fascinating. The next BCB is scheduled to take place on 4–5 October 2027.
